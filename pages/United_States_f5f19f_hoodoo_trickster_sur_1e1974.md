## Introduction

African American Hoodoo and trickster folklore are often discussed separately, but historically they worked together as cultural tools of survival. Hoodoo, also known as conjure or rootwork, developed as a tradition of protection, healing, spiritual power and practical problem-solving among African Americans, especially under slavery and segregation. Trickster tales, meanwhile, used humour, coded lessons and clever heroes to show how apparently powerless people could survive systems stacked against them. Together, these traditions preserved memory, offered hope, taught strategy and expressed resistance in ways that were often difficult for outsiders to recognise. ## Conjure, Rootwork and Protection

Hoodoo is a folk tradition rooted in African spiritual knowledge that developed in the American South. It drew on West and Central African beliefs while also incorporating local plants, Indigenous herbal knowledge and elements of Christianity. Practitioners used roots, herbs, prayers, charms and ritual actions for healing, protection, justice and guidance. Unlike a formal organised religion, Hoodoo functioned as a flexible body of community knowledge passed through families, neighbours and respected practitioners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoodoo_%28spirituality%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hoodoo (spirituality</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 1, 2026 — Hoodoo, frequently associated with conjure, is a compilation of religious beliefs and practices, centered on showing gratit...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 2026</span></span></span>

For many African Americans living under slavery, Hoodoo was not simply a supernatural belief system. It was a practical response to insecurity and violence. Historical accounts describe conjure traditions being used for protection, emotional resilience, healing and, in some cases, resistance against enslavers. The tradition offered a sense of agency in circumstances where legal and political power were largely denied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://festival.si.edu/blog/ginseng-hoodoo-african-american-traditions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: festival.si.edu">[si.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">festival.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Folklife FestivalGinseng, Hoodoo, and the Magic of Upholding African...Feb 23, 2021 — Hoodoo was created out of necessity by...</span></span></span>

A key feature of Hoodoo folklore is the belief that knowledge itself can be protective. Knowing which plants heal, which charms guard against harm, or which rituals strengthen courage became part of a wider survival culture. Stories about powerful roots, protective bags, ancestors and gifted conjurers circulated alongside practical herbal knowledge. In folklore terms, Hoodoo functioned as both a spiritual practice and a storytelling tradition, with narratives carrying lessons about danger, justice and endurance. One of the most famous examples is High John the Conqueror. In folklore he appears as a clever trickster hero who repeatedly outwits those who attempt to dominate him. At the same time, the name refers to a root used in Hoodoo practice. The overlap is significant: the folklore hero and the ritual object both symbolise resilience, luck and the refusal to surrender hope.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/high-john-conqueror" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ebsco.com">[EBSCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ebsco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High John the Conqueror &#124; Religion and PhilosophyHigh John the Conqueror is an example of African American folklore, which is the or...</span></span></span>

## Trickster Tales and Reversal

If Hoodoo addressed survival through ritual and spiritual knowledge, trickster stories addressed it through imagination and wit.

The most famous African American trickster is Brer Rabbit. Small, vulnerable and physically weaker than his opponents, he survives by intelligence rather than strength. His enemies are often larger, stronger and seemingly more powerful. Yet story after story ends with the rabbit escaping, reversing the situation or making his rivals defeat themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/tserve/freedom/1865-1917/essays/trickster.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalhumanitiescenter.org">[nationalhumanitiescenter.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalhumanitiescenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Humanities CenterThe Trickster in African American Literatureby F Douglass — For example, in one African American folktale, Brer...</span></span></span>

These stories were not merely entertainment. Folklorists and historians have long noted that audiences living under slavery could recognise themselves in the trickster figure. The rabbit's victories suggested that cleverness, patience and adaptability could sometimes overcome overwhelming power. Adults told such stories to children, but they also carried messages for entire communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.searchablemuseum.com/to-grow/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: searchablemuseum.com">[searchablemuseum.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">searchablemuseum.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Searchable MuseumTo Grow &#124; National Museum of African American History &amp;...Adults told folktales about characters like Brer Rabbit, an A...</span></span></span>

The character has deep African roots. Hare and rabbit tricksters appear across parts of Africa, where they often survive through cunning rather than force. Enslaved Africans carried these storytelling traditions across the Atlantic. In North America the stories changed, absorbing local influences and developing specifically African American meanings, but the central theme remained remarkably consistent: the weak are not necessarily powerless.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.loc.gov/folklife/2015/07/tracking-tricksters-in-washington-dc/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.loc.gov">[loc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">tracking tricksters in washington dc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressTracking Tricksters in Washington, DC &#124; Folklife TodayJul 15, 2015 — The cunning and duplicitous Brer Rabbit trick...</span></span></span>

A famous example is the tale in which Brer Rabbit convinces Brer Fox to rescue him from a well by pretending a reflection is a block of cheese. The stronger character becomes trapped while the weaker one escapes. The story is comic, but the lesson is serious: apparent authority can be manipulated, and intelligence can create opportunities where none seem to exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/tserve/freedom/1865-1917/essays/trickster.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalhumanitiescenter.org">[National Humanities Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalhumanitiescenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Humanities CenterThe Trickster in African American Literatureby F Douglass — For example, in one African American folktale, Brer...</span></span></span>

This logic of reversal appears throughout African American folklore. Tricksters often win not because they are morally perfect but because they understand systems of power better than their opponents. Their victories are temporary, humorous and sometimes morally ambiguous, yet they offer an imaginative world in which domination is never absolute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/high-john-conqueror" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ebsco.com">[ebsco.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ebsco.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">High John the Conqueror &#124; Religion and PhilosophyHigh John the Conqueror is an example of African American folklore, which is the or...</span></span></span>

## Why Survival Matters More Than Magic

Modern audiences sometimes focus on whether Hoodoo rituals "worked" or whether trickster stories were literally believed. Folklore studies ask a different question: what social work did these traditions perform?

Both Hoodoo and trickster tales helped people navigate conditions of uncertainty. They offered explanations, emotional support, practical knowledge and cultural continuity. Under slavery, families could be separated, literacy could be restricted and official institutions could be hostile. Oral tradition therefore became a crucial way of preserving identity and transmitting knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.storytellingcenter.net/blog/if-youre-not-strong-you-better-be-smart-a-short-history-of-brer-rabbit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: storytellingcenter.net">[storytellingcenter.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">storytellingcenter.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">International Storytelling Center A Short History of Br&#x27;er Rabbit</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Storytelling CenterA Short History of Br&#x27;er RabbitMay 28, 2025 — But the history of Br&#x27;er Rabbit goes back much further tha...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2025</span></span></span>

Seen this way, Hoodoo and trickster folklore share a common mechanism. Each creates a sense that survival is possible even when circumstances appear hopeless. In Hoodoo, protection comes through spiritual knowledge, roots, ancestors and ritual action. In trickster stories, protection comes through quick thinking, humour and adaptability. The methods differ, but the underlying cultural message is similar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://etd.auburn.edu/bitstream/handle/10415/5197/Arielle%20MA%20thesis%202016.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: etd.auburn.edu">[auburn.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">etd.auburn.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Arielle MA thesis 2016</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Auburn University ETDHoodoo in the Gulf Coastby A Evans · 2016 · Cited by 1 — Folklore and hoodoo taught African Americans that in order...</span></span></span>

The traditions also travelled beyond the rural South. During the Great Migration, African Americans carried Hoodoo practices, stories and beliefs into cities across the United States. New environments produced new variations, but the traditions continued to evolve rather than disappear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.magicintheunitedstates.com/episode-5-transcript" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: magicintheunitedstates.com">[Magic in the United States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">magicintheunitedstates.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Magic in the United StatesSeason 1, Episode 5 - Rootwork and the Great MigrationWe&#x27;ll uncover the incredible cultural remixing of Hoodoo...</span></span></span>

## Stereotypes, Horror and Respectful Reading

One challenge in understanding Hoodoo today is the long history of distortion. Popular culture has frequently portrayed African American conjure traditions as sinister, exotic or frightening. Films, pulp fiction and sensational journalism often reduced a complex body of healing, protection and community knowledge to stereotypes about curses and dark magic. Scholars of African American religion have repeatedly noted that these portrayals obscure the tradition's actual historical role. Trickster stories have faced a different problem. Many readers first encountered Brer Rabbit through the nineteenth-century publications of Joel Chandler Harris. Although those books preserved important material, they filtered African American oral traditions through a white Southern writer whose work remains controversial. Modern scholars therefore distinguish between the older Black oral traditions and later literary adaptations that sometimes reflected nostalgic views of the Old South.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/uncle-remus-tales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: georgiaencyclopedia.org">[georgiaencyclopedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">georgiaencyclopedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on georgiaencyclopedia.org.</span></span></span>

A respectful reading recognises both realities. Hoodoo is neither a fantasy invention nor a simple catalogue of magical practices. It is a historical folk tradition shaped by survival, adaptation and cultural creativity. Likewise, trickster tales are not merely children's animal stories. They are sophisticated narratives about power, vulnerability and resistance, carrying memories of communities that learned to endure through intelligence, humour and imagination.
