## Introduction

Among the most striking ritual objects associated with the Kongo peoples of western Democratic Republic of the Congo are the nail-covered figures often displayed in museums under names such as *nkisi nkondi* or “power figures”. To many visitors they look intimidating or even sinister, but within Kongo belief and ritual practice they were primarily protective objects. They were used to heal, enforce agreements, expose wrongdoing, settle disputes, guard communities and confront harmful spiritual forces. Far from being simple statues, they were understood as active spiritual instruments whose power depended on ritual specialists, sacred ingredients and community participation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smarthistory.org/nkisi-nkondi-kongo-people/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smarthistory.org">[smarthistory.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smarthistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples - SmarthistoryNkisi nkondi figures are highly recognizable through an accumulation...</span></span></span>

## What Do Nkisi and Minkisi Mean?

The word *nkisi* refers broadly to a spiritually empowered object. The plural form is *minkisi*. A nkisi might take the form of a carved figure, a container, a bundle of medicines or another object prepared by a ritual specialist known as a *nganga*. The physical object itself was not considered powerful merely because it existed; it became effective through ritual preparation and the addition of spiritually significant materials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dma.org/art/collection/object/3285325" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dma.org">[dma.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dma.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dallas Museum of ArtDallas Museum of ArtThe latter type of nkisi is called a power figure. ## Why Are Kongo Power Figures Covered in Nails?

The feature that most fascinates modern viewers is the dense covering of nails, blades and metal pegs driven into many figures. Popular descriptions sometimes suggest that the nails were acts of destruction or torture, but Kongo ritual traditions gave them a very different meaning.

Each insertion marked a specific moment when the figure's power was called into action. A nail might be driven in during the making of an oath, the settlement of a dispute, a request for protection or an appeal for justice. The metal object served as a visible record that the nkisi had been activated. Over time, a heavily studded figure represented a long history of interventions and obligations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/tr-TR/objects/2957" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brooklynmuseum.org">[brooklynmuseum.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brooklynmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Brooklyn Museum Nkisi Nkondi (Power Figure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Brooklyn MuseumNkisi Nkondi (Power Figure) - Brooklyn MuseumThis is a power figure made by a Kongo artist. ## How Nkisi Nkondi Figures Were Activated

The carved wooden figure was only one element in a larger ritual system. Kongo specialists prepared empowering substances known as *bilongo*, combinations of plant, animal and mineral materials chosen for specific purposes. These substances were often placed in cavities within the figure's head, abdomen or attached containers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dma.org/art/collection/object/3285325" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dma.org">[dma.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dma.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Dallas Museum of ArtDallas Museum of ArtThe latter type of nkisi is called a power figure. Many figures include a mirrored surface covering the abdominal cavity. According to Kongo interpretations recorded by researchers and museums, the reflective surface symbolised a connection to the spirit world and enabled the figure to perceive hidden threats or enemies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smarthistory.org/nkisi-nkondi-kongo-people/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smarthistory.org">[Smarthistory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smarthistory.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Power Figure (Nkisi Nkondi), Kongo peoples - SmarthistoryNkisi nkondi figures are highly recognizable through an accumulation...</span></span></span>

Activation involved the work of the *nganga*, who prepared the figure, invoked spiritual forces and directed its use. Historical accounts describe various methods of awakening or arousing a nkisi's power, including ritual speech, the insertion of nails and, in some cases, the use of gunpowder explosions to attract the figure's attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.si.edu/object/nmafa" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: si.edu">[Smithsonian Institution]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian InstitutionMale figureThe nganga commonly aroused the attention of the nkisi nkondi by exploding gunpowder in front of it. Because of this process, Kongo communities generally did not regard a power figure as an artwork in the modern museum sense. Its effectiveness depended on relationships among ritual specialists, ancestral forces, community expectations and the materials placed within it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://umma.umich.edu/objects/power-figure-2005-1-191/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: umma.umich.edu">[umich.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">umma.umich.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">University of Michigan Museum of ArtArt Object: Power FigureThrough the intervention of a ritual expert (nganga), an nkisi, or power figu...</span></span></span>

## Protection, Justice and Community Life

Nkisi nkondi figures are often described as protective objects, but their role went beyond simple defence against supernatural dangers. They operated within systems of social trust and communal justice.

When agreements were made before a nkisi, participants believed that breaking an oath could bring consequences from the spiritual forces associated with the figure. This gave the nkisi a role similar to a witness, guardian and enforcer combined. ## How Nkisi Figures Are Understood Today

Today, nkisi nkondi figures are recognised simultaneously as ritual objects, historical records, masterpieces of Central African art and symbols of Kongo cultural heritage. Their image appears frequently in books, exhibitions and online discussions because the nail-covered surfaces are visually unforgettable.

Yet the most important point is that the nails were never the whole story. Behind every blade and peg lay a request, an oath, a dispute, a healing effort or a protective act. For Kongo communities, these figures were not merely representations of power; they were tools through which power was believed to operate in the world. Understanding that context transforms them from mysterious museum objects into evidence of a rich tradition linking protection, justice, memory and spiritual belief across western DR Congo and the wider Kongo cultural region.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.slam.org/collection/objects/59175/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slam.org">[slam.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slam.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Power Figure (nkisi nkondi)Each iron blade or nail across the torso attests to specific moments when the nganga, a ritual specialist...</span></span></span>
