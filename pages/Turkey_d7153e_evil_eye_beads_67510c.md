## Introduction

Few symbols are more closely associated with modern [Turkey]({{ 'turkey/' | relative_url }}) than the blue evil-eye bead. It appears on front doors, in taxis, above office desks, on baby clothes, in jewellery, and among the souvenirs sold in almost every tourist district. Yet the bead is not simply a decorative national emblem. It belongs to a much older and wider folk belief: the idea that envy, excessive admiration, or a hostile glance can cause harm. In Turkish tradition, the evil-eye bead serves as a visible defence against that danger, turning an invisible threat into something people can recognise and manage in everyday life. The popularity of the bead reveals how Turkish folklore operates not only through stories but through daily habits, objects, and rituals that remain meaningful even in a highly modern society.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.turkishtextbook.com/nazar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: turkishtextbook.com">[turkishtextbook.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">turkishtextbook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turkish TextbookNazar: the Evil Eye in Turkish CultureThe evil eye, also known as “nazar” in Turkish, is a concept deeply embedded in Tur...</span></span></span>

## The Harmful Gaze and the Fear of Envy

At the heart of the tradition lies a simple but powerful idea: good fortune attracts attention, and attention can sometimes be dangerous. In Turkish belief, a person who is unusually beautiful, successful, healthy, wealthy, or fortunate may become vulnerable to the harmful effects of envy. The danger does not necessarily come from deliberate malice. Even sincere admiration can be thought to carry an unintended destructive force if it is not balanced with caution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.turkishtextbook.com/nazar/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: turkishtextbook.com">[turkishtextbook.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">turkishtextbook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Turkish TextbookNazar: the Evil Eye in Turkish CultureThe evil eye, also known as “nazar” in Turkish, is a concept deeply embedded in Tur...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why evil-eye beliefs are often linked to moments when people feel especially exposed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* The birth of a baby.
* A new house or business.
* A wedding.
* The purchase of a car.
* Public success or praise.

</div>

In folk thinking, these are moments when others might admire what someone has gained, creating a risk of attracting harmful attention. The evil-eye bead functions as a protective barrier between the admired object and the dangerous gaze directed toward it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.insideoutinistanbul.com/turkish-superstitions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: insideoutinistanbul.com">[insideoutinistanbul.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">insideoutinistanbul.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Inside Out In IstanbulTurkish superstitions – truth or fiction?The belief in the evil eye is very strong in Turkey, even in the city, but...</span></span></span>

The belief itself is much older than the modern bead. Evil-eye traditions are found across the Mediterranean and Middle East, and Turkish culture absorbed and reshaped these ideas through centuries of interaction across Anatolia, the Balkans, and neighbouring regions. Some scholars also note parallels with older Turkic beliefs that attributed unusual power to the human eye and to unseen forces affecting health and fortune.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kureansiklopedi.com/en/detay/the-evil-eye-belief-in-turkish-culture-34d94" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kureansiklopedi.com">[Küre Encyclopedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kureansiklopedi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the evil eye belief in turkish culture 34d94</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Küre EncyclopediaThe Evil Eye Belief in Turkish Culture9 Dec 2025 — The belief in nazar is present in the pre-Islamic shamanistic beliefs...</span></span></span>

## Why the Bead Looks Like an Eye

The famous blue bead is not intended to represent the victim of the evil eye. Instead, it symbolically confronts the threatening gaze with another eye.

According to common explanations within the tradition, the bead acts as a decoy. The harmful look is drawn toward the amulet rather than toward the person, child, animal, home, or possession being protected. Some versions of the belief describe the bead as absorbing negative energy; others emphasise its role in reflecting danger away from the target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crees.ku.edu/nazar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crees.ku.edu">[ku.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crees.ku.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alerts Nazar Boncuğu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KU AlertsNazar Boncuğu - ku crees - The University of Kansas...</span></span></span>

The colour blue has acquired particular importance. Explanations vary, but many accounts connect blue with protection and defence. Another frequently repeated interpretation points out that light-coloured eyes were historically less common in parts of the eastern Mediterranean, leading some communities to associate striking blue eyes with unusual powers and therefore with the danger the amulet was designed to counter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crees.ku.edu/nazar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crees.ku.edu">[KU Alerts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crees.ku.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alerts Nazar Boncuğu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KU AlertsNazar Boncuğu - ku crees - The University of Kansas...</span></span></span>

An especially widespread folk belief holds that a bead that cracks or breaks has fulfilled its protective role by absorbing a powerful burst of harmful energy. When this happens, the damaged bead is often replaced rather than repaired.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crees.ku.edu/nazar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crees.ku.edu">[KU Alerts]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crees.ku.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alerts Nazar Boncuğu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KU AlertsNazar Boncuğu - ku crees - The University of Kansas...</span></span></span>

## Beads, Babies, Homes and Daily Protection

What makes the Turkish evil-eye tradition distinctive is not merely belief in misfortune but the way protection becomes part of ordinary life.

Newborn children are among the most common recipients of protection. Families may attach a small bead to a baby's clothing, place one near a cradle, or display one outside a hospital room. The practice reflects the widespread idea that infants are particularly vulnerable to envy because they attract praise and attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://crees.ku.edu/nazar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: crees.ku.edu">[ku.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">crees.ku.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Alerts Nazar Boncuğu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">KU AlertsNazar Boncuğu - ku crees - The University of Kansas...</span></span></span>

Homes are another major focus. Evil-eye symbols are frequently placed:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Near entrances.
* In living rooms.
* On walls.
* In gardens.
* On household decorations.

</div>

The placement turns the bead into a symbolic guardian watching over the household. Vehicles, shops, offices, and workplaces are treated in a similar way, especially when they represent a significant investment or source of family income.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://madeinturkeytours.com/history-meaning-turkish-evil-eye/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: madeinturkeytours.com">[madeinturkeytours.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">madeinturkeytours.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Made in Turkey ToursThe History and the Meaning of the Turkish Evil Eye5 May 2026 — To thwart the evil eye, the Turkish people created th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2026</span></span></span>

Protection can also be woven into gifts. Giving an evil-eye charm to newlyweds, new homeowners, students, or someone beginning a new job expresses goodwill while acknowledging the possibility of envy from others. The object therefore carries both emotional and protective meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.turkeyhomes.com/blog/post/the-blue-turkish-evil-eye-nazar-amulet-meaning-and-should-i-wear-it" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: turkeyhomes.com">[Turkey Homes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">turkeyhomes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the blue turkish evil eye nazar amulet meaning and should i wear it</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Not that the actual item is evil, but folklore promotes it as something to ward off the gaze of misfortune...Read more...</span></span></span>

These practices show how Turkish folklore often operates through small preventive actions rather than dramatic rituals. The evil eye is not usually imagined as a monster or spirit. It is a risk embedded in social life itself, arising from ordinary human emotions such as jealousy, admiration, competition, and pride.

## A Living Craft Tradition

The bead's cultural importance extends beyond belief into craftsmanship. Traditional evil-eye beads are handmade from molten glass, and certain communities in western Turkey became particularly known for producing them. This heritage dimension matters because the bead is not merely bought and sold. For many people it remains part of a cultural system linking family traditions, local craftsmanship, and ideas about protection.

## Tourist Souvenir, Living Charm or Both?

Visitors to Turkey often encounter the evil-eye bead first as a souvenir. It appears on keyrings, magnets, jewellery, ceramics, textiles, and countless decorative items. Its visibility can make it seem like a marketing symbol rather than a living tradition.
