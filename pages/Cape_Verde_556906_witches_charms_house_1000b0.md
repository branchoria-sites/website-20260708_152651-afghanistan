## Introduction

Cape Verdean witch stories are less about dramatic sabbaths or famous national sorceresses than about the vulnerability of ordinary families. In the fragments that survive from oral tradition, older folklore collections, household memories, and modern retellings, witches appear as threats to babies, health, good fortune, and domestic peace. The central fear was often not a monster in the wilderness but a harmful force entering the home through envy, illness, bad luck, or supernatural attack. Protective prayers, crosses, charms, vigils, and ritual acts were therefore aimed at defending the household itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/534936" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2 Graves unpaid for may be dug up after six months to accommodate others. ## What Did Cape Verdeans Fear at Night?

### Witch belief and local variation

References to witches in Cape Verde generally use ideas inherited from the Portuguese-speaking world while also reflecting African-derived understandings of spiritual danger and misfortune. Folklore sources describe fear of *feitiçaria*—witchcraft or harmful magic—as a persistent feature of popular belief. Misfortunes that could not easily be explained, including illness, weakness, bad luck, or family troubles, might be attributed to supernatural causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://salcaboverde.com/cape-verdean-folklore-tales-and-legends-of-the-islands/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: salcaboverde.com">[Sal Cabo Verde]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">salcaboverde.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cape verdean folklore tales and legends of the islands</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sal Cabo VerdeCape Verdean Folklore: Tales and Legends of the Islands20 Jan 2021 — Fear of witchcraft (feitiçaria) ran so deep that even...</span></span></span>

One recurring theme concerns children. Newborns and infants were widely regarded as especially vulnerable. Older traditions describe dangers posed by witches or malevolent supernatural influences during the first days of life. The threat was not usually presented as spectacular evil but as a hidden attack on a child's health, destiny, or spiritual wellbeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cabo-verde.cv/cape-verdean-superstitions-a-guide-to-local-customs-beliefs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cabo-verde.cv">[Cabo Verde]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cabo-verde.cv</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cape verdean superstitions a guide to local customs beliefs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">witches) who might try to change the child&#x27;s destiny. ## Crosses, Charms and Home Protection

The response to witchcraft fears was practical. Protection mattered more than elaborate stories about witches themselves.

Christian symbols played an important role. Prayers, blessings, and the placement of crosses within domestic spaces were common ways of defending the household. A notable passage recorded by folklorist Elsie Clews Parsons refers to a woman being instructed to "tie up" a witch through prayer, suggesting a belief that spoken religious formulas could restrain harmful supernatural power.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/534936" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2 Graves unpaid for may be dug up after six months to accommodate others. Modern accounts describe protective beads and amulets attached to infants' clothing, blankets, or cradles. ## The Household as a Place of Risk

Many Cape Verdean supernatural beliefs revolve around moments when the normal boundaries of the home were thought to weaken.

Particular concern surrounded:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* The birth of a child.
* Periods of illness or unexplained weakness.
* Death and mourning.
* Times of social tension, jealousy, or family conflict.

In these situations, neighbours, relatives, godparents, healers, and religious figures could all become part of a protective network. Rather than separating medicine, religion, and folklore into distinct categories, traditional practice often blended them. ## Why the Evidence Is Fragmentary

Readers looking for a famous Cape Verdean witch legend often discover that the evidence is surprisingly thin. There are several reasons for this.

First, Cape Verde's folklore developed largely through oral transmission. Stories remained in families and communities rather than being systematically written down.
