## Introduction

[Japan]({{ 'why-japans-old-stories-still-feel-alive/' | relative_url }})’s legends are often inseparable from place. A ghost story may belong to a particular bridge, a mountain spirit to a specific valley, or a famous folk hero to a named shrine and ancient road. While many countries preserve folklore in books, Japan has also preserved it in landscapes. Travellers can still visit streams associated with water spirits, villages linked to famous storytellers, museums devoted to supernatural beings, and shrine complexes that claim connections to beloved legends. ## Sacred Landscapes and the Geography of Belief

Japanese folklore has long been shaped by the idea that particular locations possess unusual significance. Mountains, waterfalls, caves, giant trees and remote coastlines were often treated as places where the human world touched something beyond it. Rather than separating religion, local history and storytelling, communities frequently blended them together.

This helps explain why regional folklore remains so strong. A tale survives not only because people remember it but because they continue to encounter the place associated with it. A stream linked to a water spirit remains visible. A shrine connected to a legendary hero still hosts ceremonies. A mountain associated with supernatural visitors continues to dominate the horizon. The landscape acts as a memory device, preserving stories across generations.

Across Japan, folklore destinations often sit at the intersection of heritage preservation and tourism. Visitors are encouraged not merely to hear a story but to walk through it. The result is a distinctive folklore map in which local identity is anchored to particular locations rather than to a single national mythology.

## Tono: The Landscape of Living Folklore

No place is more closely associated with Japanese folk tradition than Tono in Iwate Prefecture. The area became famous through *The Legends of Tono*, published in the early twentieth century by folklorist Kunio Yanagita using stories collected from local storyteller Sasaki Kizen. The book helped establish folklore studies in Japan and turned Tono into a symbolic homeland of traditional storytelling.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web-japan.org/trends/11_food/jfd130121.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web-japan.org">[Web Japan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web-japan.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Web JapanTONO &#124; Food &amp; Travel &#124; Trends in Japan21 Jan 2013 — The city of Tono in Iwate Prefecture, northeastern Japan, is famous as the h...</span></span></span>

The setting is important. Tono lies in a mountain-ringed basin whose isolated villages preserved oral traditions long after they disappeared elsewhere. Local stories describe mountain spirits, mysterious women, [ghosts]({{ 'ghosts-3c6a88/' | relative_url }}), talking animals and encounters with strange beings. Even today the town presents itself as a place where the world of legend remains close to everyday life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://tonojikan.jp/ml/english/welcome/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tonojikan.jp">[遠野時間｜遠野市観光情報サイト]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tonojikan.jp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">遠野時間｜遠野市観光情報サイトようこそ-Tono Tourism Association｜Tono JapanTono is a city surrounded by deep mountain range, located in the southeastern part...</span></span></span>

## Oga and the Fearsome Visitors of Winter

If Tono represents storytelling, the Oga Peninsula in Akita Prefecture represents living ritual folklore. Oga is famous for the Namahage, masked figures who visit homes on New Year's Eve, shouting warnings to lazy children and encouraging good behaviour. ## Miyoshi and the Modern Museum of Monsters

Further south, Miyoshi in Hiroshima Prefecture demonstrates another way folklore landscapes evolve. Instead of relying primarily on surviving ritual, Miyoshi has become a centre for preserving and interpreting supernatural traditions through museums and exhibitions.

The city is associated with famous monster narratives that circulated widely during the Edo period. This connection helped inspire the establishment of the Miyoshi Mononoke Museum, officially the Yumoto Koichi Memorial Japan [Yokai]({{ 'yokai/' | relative_url }}) Museum. The institution houses thousands of objects related to supernatural beings and serves as one of Japan’s most important public centres for the study and display of folklore creatures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dive-hiroshima.com/en/explore/364/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dive-hiroshima.com">[dive-hiroshima.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dive-hiroshima.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">〖公式〗広島の観光・旅行情報サイト Dive!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HiroshimaMiyoshi Mononoke Memorial Japanese Youkai MuseumThe museum was opened in this historically rich area, with the donation of an en...</span></span></span>

## Momotaro, Okayama and the Question of Origins

Perhaps no regional folklore claim is more famous than Okayama's connection to Momotaro, the hero born from a peach who defeats a band of ogres with the help of animal companions.
