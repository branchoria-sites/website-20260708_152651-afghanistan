## Introduction

[Vanuatu]({{ 'why-vanuatus-folklore-lives-island-by/' | relative_url }}) contains some of the Pacific’s most striking examples of landscapes that seem to “remember” stories. Two famous cases—Chief Roi Mata’s domain in central Vanuatu and the traditions surrounding the lost island of Kuwae—show how oral history can become attached to specific places and remain meaningful for centuries. In both cases, stories are not floating legends detached from geography. They are tied to caves, islands, burial grounds, coastlines and volcanic scars that people can still point to today. Archaeology, geology and community memory have often reinforced one another rather than competing, making these traditions important examples of how folklore, history and place can overlap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1280/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreChief Roi Mata&#x27;s DomainChief Roi Mata&#x27;s Domain is the first site to be inscribed in Vanuatu. ## Roi Mata as Oral Tradition and Heritage

The story of Roi Mata centres on a powerful chief remembered across parts of central Vanuatu. According to oral traditions, he brought rival groups together, established peace and social reforms, and became one of the most influential figures in local memory. His death is remembered as a major turning point, and stories about him remained active long after the events themselves passed from living memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1280/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreChief Roi Mata&#x27;s DomainChief Roi Mata&#x27;s Domain is the first site to be inscribed in Vanuatu. It consists of t...</span></span></span>

What makes Roi Mata especially important is that the tradition is anchored to a landscape rather than a single monument. The UNESCO World Heritage site known as Chief Roi Mata’s Domain includes three separate places: the location associated with his residence on Efate, the cave on Lelepa where he is said to have died, and the burial site on Artok Island. These places are linked by oral narratives that have been preserved through customary authority and local stewardship.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1280/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreChief Roi Mata&#x27;s DomainChief Roi Mata&#x27;s Domain is the first site to be inscribed in Vanuatu. The relationship between story and place became internationally famous because oral tradition helped guide archaeological discovery. In the 1960s, archaeologist José Garanger worked with local knowledge holders and used traditional accounts to locate Roi Mata’s burial site. Excavations revealed a remarkable mass burial broadly consistent with the remembered narratives. For many people in Vanuatu, this was not simply archaeology proving folklore; it was evidence that oral history had preserved knowledge across centuries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roi_Mata" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roi Mata</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roi Mata</span></span></span>

The site’s World Heritage recognition explicitly emphasises this continuing connection between landscape, memory and customary authority. The significance lies not only in ancient remains but also in the fact that communities still associate these locations with Roi Mata’s legacy and continue to observe traditional restrictions and respect connected to them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/1488/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Decision</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDecision - 32 COM 8B.27The authenticity of Chief Roi Mata&#x27;s Domain lies in the continuing association of the...</span></span></span>

## Kuwae and Volcanic Memory

If Roi Mata represents remembered authority, Kuwae represents remembered catastrophe.

Kuwae is associated with a massive volcanic eruption in the fifteenth century. According to traditions from the Shepherd Islands region, a large inhabited landmass once existed where sea now lies between islands such as Epi and Tongoa. Stories describe dramatic destruction, the disappearance of land, migrations, and the reshaping of communities after a catastrophic eruption.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13617-023-00138-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[springer.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evidence at source for the mid-fifteenth century eruption of...by C Ballard · 2023 · Cited by 8 — The oral traditions suggest th...</span></span></span>

For many years these accounts were treated largely as legend. Modern geological and archaeological research, however, has increasingly suggested that the traditions preserve memories of a genuine large-scale volcanic disaster. Studies of the region have linked oral accounts to evidence of a major mid-fifteenth-century eruption that transformed the landscape and separated areas that traditions describe as formerly connected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13617-023-00138-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: link.springer.com">[springer.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">link.springer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Evidence at source for the mid-fifteenth century eruption of...by C Ballard · 2023 · Cited by 8 — The oral traditions suggest th...</span></span></span>

Researchers have been particularly interested in the detail retained within the stories. Oral narratives describe the loss of territory, population movements and the social consequences of the eruption. Archaeological work on Epi and Tongoa has explored how communities recovered and reorganised after the event, while oral traditions continue to provide clues about settlement history and migration routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/kuwae-epi-and-tongoa-islands-transformations-of-a-volcanic-landsc/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchportalplus.anu.edu.au">[edu.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchportalplus.anu.edu.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Australian National UniversityKuwae, Epi and Tongoa Islands: Transformations of a volcanic...by R Henderson · 2025 · Cited by 6 — Th...</span></span></span>

The Kuwae traditions are not a simple eyewitness report preserved unchanged for six centuries. They have been retold, adapted and interpreted by many generations. Yet scholars increasingly regard them as valuable historical sources because they preserve memories of environmental upheaval that can be compared with physical evidence from the landscape itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/26907876" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[jstor.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Lizard in the Volcano: Narratives of the Kuwae Eruptionby C Ballard · 2020 · Cited by 30 — In Tongoan oral tradition, the erupti...</span></span></span>

### Remembering a Lost Island

One reason Kuwae fascinates folklorists is that it blurs the boundary between mythic and historical memory.

The stories describe:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Land that no longer exists in its former form.
* Communities forced to relocate.
* Earthquakes, sea disturbances and volcanic destruction.
* Ancestors navigating a transformed world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://os.pennds.org/archaeobib_filestore/pdf_articles/BIPPA/2006_26_Hoffmann.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: os.pennds.org">[pennds.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">os.pennds.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FURTHER CONSEQUENCES OF THE KUWAE ERUPTION...by A Hoffmann · Cited by 21 — These oral traditions describe migrations of people between E...</span></span></span>

</div>

Rather than being attached to a standing monument, the memory is attached to an absence. The sea itself becomes part of the story. Looking across the waters between islands, people are reminded not of what remains but of what disappeared.

## How Place Turns Story into Authority

Taken together, Roi Mata and Kuwae reveal a recurring pattern in Vanuatu’s traditional culture: stories gain authority when they are tied to specific landscapes.

A cave associated with a chief’s death, a burial island protected by customary rules, or a stretch of ocean believed to cover a vanished homeland all provide physical reference points that help stories endure. The landscape becomes a form of cultural storage. Each visit, ceremony, voyage or retelling reconnects people to the narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/1488/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whc.unesco.org">[UNESCO World Heritage Centre]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whc.unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Heritage Centre Decision</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNESCO World Heritage CentreDecision - 32 COM 8B.27The authenticity of Chief Roi Mata&#x27;s Domain lies in the continuing association of the...</span></span></span>

This does not mean every traditional account can be read as straightforward history. Folklore often reshapes events, emphasises moral lessons and incorporates symbolic elements. Yet the examples of Roi Mata and Kuwae show why many researchers now approach oral traditions with greater respect.
